How to choose repair shop software

Repair shop software runs the whole job: check-in, the estimate, the customer's approval, parts, payment and the technician's pay. Here's what to check before you pick one — whichever vendor you choose.

Updated 23 October 2026 · about 5 minutes to read

The checklist

  1. Start with your intake
  2. Check how customers approve work
  3. Follow a part from order to job
  4. Look at money and people together
  5. Read the pricing page carefully
  6. Make sure your data can leave
  7. Plan the switch
  8. Be clear about AI
  9. Comparing specific products

01 Start with your intake

The software should ask for what your trade needs the moment a job comes in: the IMEI or serial for phones and computers, the VIN or plate and mileage for vehicles, engine hours for small engines, the hull ID and each engine's serial for boats. Ask to see a real check-in screen for your trade, not a generic ticket.

02 Check how customers approve work

Approval is where repairs stall and where disputes start. Look for approval on the customer's phone, a signature, and the ability to approve some lines and decline others. Ask what happens when you find more work mid-repair: does the customer get a clear choice, and is declined work kept for next time?

03 Follow a part from order to job

Order a part from a job, receive it, and see where its cost ends up. Good software charges the part to the right job automatically, so the job's margin is right without anyone re-typing it. If you sell retail too, check the POS and stock work from the same inventory.

04 Look at money and people together

Payroll, technician commission, the cash drawer and accounting sync are often separate tools. If they're in one system, commissions come straight from closed jobs and refunds can claw them back. Check whether it syncs with QuickBooks or Xero if your accountant uses them.

05 Read the pricing page carefully

Compare the total for your team, not the headline price. Check: how many people are included and what each extra person costs; what each extra location costs; whether prices are monthly or only quarterly or annual; setup or data-migration fees; and which features are paid add-ons. A free trial you can use without talking to sales is a good sign.

06 Make sure your data can leave

You should be able to export customers, jobs, stock and finances to CSV or Excel at any time, including after you cancel. Ask the vendor to show you the export.

07 Plan the switch

Ask who moves your data and what it costs. Many vendors import an export from your current system or spreadsheets; some charge for it. A single-location shop should expect to be running within a day or two.

08 Be clear about AI

AI can draft estimates, summarise a job or suggest a reply. Check that a person always decides what's sent to a customer, that your data isn't pooled with other shops, and that every AI step has a manual path.

Questions

Repair shop software, answered.

What is repair shop software?

One system for everything a repair shop does: checking jobs in, estimates and customer approvals, ordering parts, the point of sale, invoices, inventory and payroll.

How much does repair shop software cost?

Published prices range widely. As of October 2026, Tekly starts at $29 a month, Orderry at $39, RepairShopr at $59.99–$69.99, RepairDesk from $79 (billed annually), Tekmetric at $179–$199 per shop and Shopmonkey at $215–$239. Compare what your whole team would pay, including extra people and locations.

Do I need special hardware?

Usually not. Most current systems, Tekly included, run in a web browser on a computer, tablet or phone and work with standard barcode scanners, label printers and receipt printers.

How long does it take to switch?

For a single-location shop, typically a day or two once your data is exported. Ask whether the vendor moves the data for you and whether they charge for it.
